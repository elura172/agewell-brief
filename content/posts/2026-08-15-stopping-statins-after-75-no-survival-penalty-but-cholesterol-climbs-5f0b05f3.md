A randomized trial finds that discontinuing statins in older adults without prior cardiovascular events does not raise three-year mortality, yet LDL rises sharply. The decision hinges on biology, not habit.

## Why this matters

Statins are the cornerstone of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease prevention, working by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase to lower hepatic cholesterol synthesis. For adults 75 and older without a history of heart attack or stroke, the balance of benefit and harm becomes murkier. Polypharmacy, muscle symptoms, and reduced physiological reserve complicate long-term use.

This trial directly addresses a common clinical dilemma: can older patients safely stop a preventive medication they may no longer need? The answer, at least over three years, appears to be yes for mortality—but the metabolic consequences are not trivial. Understanding the underlying lipid dynamics is essential for making an informed choice.

## What the trial found

In a randomized controlled trial published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, adults 75 and older with no prior heart disease or stroke were assigned to either continue or stop their statin. Over three years, all-cause mortality was similar between groups. This suggests that, in this specific population, the survival benefit of continued statin use may be minimal.

However, stopping statins led to a roughly 50% increase in LDL cholesterol—the 'bad' cholesterol that drives atheroma formation. This rise reflects the removal of hepatic LDL receptor upregulation, a direct consequence of statin action. The divergence between mortality and lipid levels highlights the complexity of cardiovascular risk in advanced age.

## How to interpret the evidence

The trial's findings are robust but limited. Three years is a short window for a chronic disease process like atherosclerosis, which develops over decades. The absence of a mortality difference does not prove equivalence in non-fatal events, such as heart attacks or strokes, which were not reported in the summary. Longer-term data are needed to assess cumulative risk.

The LDL rise is a clear biological signal. Elevated LDL is a causal factor in atherogenesis, and a 50% increase could accelerate plaque progression. Yet, in older adults with no prior events, the absolute risk may be lower than in younger populations. The decision to stop statins should weigh individual risk factors, life expectancy, and patient preferences, not just the lipid numbers.

## Practical next steps

If you are 75 or older and taking a statin for primary prevention, discuss this trial with your clinician. Do not stop abruptly—statins have pleiotropic effects, and sudden withdrawal could unmask underlying inflammation or endothelial dysfunction. A shared decision-making approach is essential.

Ask about your baseline LDL, overall cardiovascular risk, and any statin-related side effects. If you choose to stop, plan for regular lipid monitoring and revisit the decision annually. Lifestyle factors—diet, exercise, and mitochondrial health—remain foundational. As biophysicist Otto Warburg showed, cellular respiration and mitochondrial voltage are central to metabolic health, and these are influenced by diet and activity, not just pharmacology.

## Three things to remember

- Stopping statins did not increase three-year mortality in adults 75+ without prior events.
- LDL cholesterol rose about 50% in those who stopped.
- Trial results apply only to primary prevention in this age group.
