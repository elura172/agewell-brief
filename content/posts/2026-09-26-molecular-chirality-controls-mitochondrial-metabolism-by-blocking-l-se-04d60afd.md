Mirror-image stereochemistry alters cellular energy pathways as D-serine competes for mitochondrial entry, choking the one-carbon metabolism required by rapidly dividing neural progenitor and glioblastoma stem cells.

Proteins and metabolites in living systems are overwhelmingly left-handed, yet biology occasionally deploys right-handed mirror images to execute entirely different physical tasks. In the mature central nervous system, enzymes actively flip left-handed L-serine into its right-handed counterpart, D-serine, which binds NMDA receptors to propagate neural signals. To examine whether this optical isomer also alters baseline cellular chemistry, researchers tracked metabolic flux across neural cultures and cell models. Metabolomic profiling revealed that introducing D-serine sharply depletes intracellular pools of glycine and formate, direct downstream products of mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism. Molecular dynamics simulations and enzymatic assays established that D-serine does not bind or inhibit serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2, the primary enzyme driving the pathway's initial mitochondrial step. Instead, membrane transport assays demonstrated that D-serine physically competes with L-serine for entry across the mitochondrial membrane, starving the downstream enzymatic machinery of its necessary fuel.

This enantiomeric competition exerts profound functional consequences when nutrient supplies run thin. Under low L-serine conditions both in vitro and ex vivo, the influx of D-serine suppresses the proliferation of immature neural cells and glioblastoma stem cells, which rely heavily on active one-carbon pathways to replicate. Developmental tracking showed that endogenous D-serine levels remain low during early embryonic neurodevelopment and rise only as tissue matures, matching shifts in the transcriptional profiles of local metabolic enzymes. These findings reveal that stereochemistry serves as an active metabolic switch within mitochondria rather than acting as an inert byproduct of neurotransmitter synthesis. However, these mechanistic insights derive from in vitro and ex vivo models, and the precise physiological scope of D-serine-mediated mitochondrial restriction across intact mammalian tissues requires further in vivo validation.

## Key takeaways

- D-serine administration depletes intracellular glycine and formate pools by blocking the initial enzymatic steps of mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism.
- Molecular dynamics and enzyme assays prove D-serine does not directly inhibit serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2, but rather cuts off its substrate supply.
- Endogenous D-serine concentrations scale upward during neural maturation, matching a developmental shift in how cells process serine enantiomers.
