A new study finds that work-related stress is associated with significant sleep disturbances in people over 50, with many experiencing persistent issues.



## Three things to remember

- Nearly 70% of participants had persistent, clinically significant sleep problems.
- Almost 92% met the threshold for a sleep disorder at least once.
- Work-related stress is linked to a range of well-being issues.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern study's association between work-related stress and persistent sleep problems in adults over 50 is mechanistically bridged by the HPA axis and autonomic nervous system. Chronic work stress elevates cortisol and catecholamines, which activate arousal-promoting brain regions (e.g., locus coeruleus, orexin neurons) and inhibit sleep-promoting areas (e.g., ventrolateral preoptic nucleus). Elevated cortisol also reduces slow-wave sleep and impairs circadian entrainment, as predicted by Selye's stress model and Borbély's two-process model. Additionally, age-related declines in melatonin secretion and circadian amplitude (as documented by Weitzman and colleagues in the 1980s) compound these effects, making older workers particularly susceptible to stress-induced insomnia and fragmented sleep.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles, such as those of the Hadza or San peoples, exhibit lower baseline cortisol and more regular sleep-wake cycles tied to natural light and physical activity. Unlike modern work environments with chronic psychosocial stressors, ancestral stressors were acute and episodic (e.g., predation, food scarcity), allowing for recovery and stress-system reset. The physical mechanism involves the HPA axis's natural diurnal rhythm: in ancestral settings, cortisol peaks in the morning and declines by night, promoting sleep onset. In contrast, chronic work stress in modern settings flattens or shifts this rhythm, delaying melatonin release and disrupting sleep. This parallel suggests that aligning work schedules with natural light, incorporating physical activity, and ensuring recovery periods could mitigate stress-induced sleep problems in older adults.
