A novel biomarker outperforms leading tests at forecasting disease progression, offering hope for earlier intervention.

## Why This Matters

For the millions of people living with Alzheimer's disease and their families, one of the most distressing questions is: 'How fast will this progress?' Current blood tests can detect amyloid plaques, a hallmark of the disease, but they often do so years before symptoms appear, leaving patients and doctors in the dark about what comes next. This uncertainty makes it difficult to plan for the future or decide on treatments.

Now, researchers funded by the National Institutes of Health have identified a new type of biomarker in the blood that may change that. Small loops of genetic material called circular RNAs (circRNAs) appear to be more dynamic than amyloid plaques, reflecting recent brain activity. Elevated levels of certain circRNAs nearly tripled a person's risk of developing Alzheimer's symptoms, suggesting they could serve as an early warning system.

This is not just about predicting the future; it's about improving clinical trials and treatments. As Dr. Richard Hodes, director of NIH's National Institute on Aging, explains, identifying patients on the verge of symptom onset could help select the right participants for trials and better determine which treatments prevent cognitive decline. That could accelerate the development of therapies that make a real difference.

## What the Study Found

The research, published in Nature Medicine, analyzed blood data from more than 1,200 people across multiple independent cohorts. The team, led by Dr. Carlos Cruchaga at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, identified a set of 34 circRNAs associated with Alzheimer's disease. They then built predictive models based on these circRNAs and compared them to models based on pTau217, the leading clinical blood biomarker for Alzheimer's.

When it came to diagnosing Alzheimer's pathology, the circRNA model performed similarly to the pTau217 model. But the circRNA model was far superior at predicting progression to symptomatic Alzheimer's. The levels of these circRNAs seemed to diverge from normal about two to four years before symptom onset. Importantly, the findings were replicated in two independent cohorts, adding to their credibility.

These results suggest that circRNAs may provide a more complete picture of the disease's trajectory. As Dr. Cruchaga notes, patients treated with amyloid-removing therapies can become pTau negative but still have Alzheimer's. Circular RNAs might offer a broader view of the disease biology, potentially helping monitor treatment response.

## How to Interpret the Findings

It's important to understand that this is early-stage research. The study is observational, meaning it shows an association between circRNA levels and Alzheimer's progression, but it does not prove that circRNAs cause the disease. The findings are promising, but they are not yet ready for clinical use. The test is not available to the public, and more research is needed to validate it in larger, more diverse populations.

The study's strength lies in its use of multiple independent cohorts and its comparison to an established biomarker. However, the predictive models were developed and tested in specific groups, and their real-world performance may vary. The authors themselves are working with commercial partners to develop clinical assays, indicating that translation to practice will take time.

For now, these findings should be viewed as a step forward in understanding Alzheimer's disease. They highlight the potential of circRNAs as a tool for predicting symptom onset, which could eventually help doctors and families plan for the future. But they do not change current diagnostic or treatment recommendations.

## Practical Next Steps

If you or a loved one are concerned about Alzheimer's disease, the best course of action is to talk with a healthcare provider. They can discuss your risk factors, order appropriate tests, and refer you to specialists if needed. While this new blood test is not yet available, existing diagnostic tools, including amyloid PET scans and cerebrospinal fluid analysis, can provide valuable information.

In the meantime, focus on what is known to support brain health: a balanced diet, regular physical activity, social engagement, and managing chronic conditions like diabetes and high blood pressure. These lifestyle factors may help reduce the risk of cognitive decline, even if they cannot prevent Alzheimer's entirely.

Stay informed about new research, but be cautious of overhyped claims. This study is a promising development, but it is not a breakthrough that will change practice overnight. As Dr. Cruchaga says, 'It's nice to have good science and models, but we're ultimately doing this to help people.' That help is on the horizon, but it requires continued research and validation.

## Three things to remember

- Circular RNAs in blood may predict Alzheimer's symptom onset.
- They outperform pTau217 in forecasting disease progression.
- Test not yet available; more research needed.
