New research in adults with HIV suggests semaglutide could influence aging markers, but experts urge caution.
## Three things to remember

- Semaglutide slowed biological aging markers in adults with HIV.
- First clinical evidence the drug may influence human aging.
- Larger studies needed before confirming anti-aging effects.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
Semaglutide activates GLP-1 receptors, which are expressed in various tissues including the brain, pancreas, and immune cells. This activation reduces oxidative stress, lowers systemic inflammation (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), and improves mitochondrial function. In HIV patients, chronic inflammation and immune activation drive epigenetic changes and telomere attrition, hallmarks of biological aging. By dampening inflammation and improving metabolic health, semaglutide may slow the accumulation of aging-related epigenetic modifications, as measured by epigenetic clocks. This provides a direct mechanistic link between metabolic regulation and aging, echoing Warburg's and Selye's insights that metabolic and stress pathways are central to cellular aging.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional lifestyle practices such as intermittent fasting and calorie restriction have long been associated with longevity. These practices enhance insulin sensitivity and reduce inflammation, similar to the effects of semaglutide. The drug mimics the metabolic benefits of fasting without the behavioral change, offering a pharmacological parallel to ancestral eating patterns. In ancestral environments, periodic food scarcity led to metabolic adaptations that promoted cellular repair and longevity. Semaglutide may activate similar pathways (e.g., AMPK, sirtuins) that are triggered by fasting, thus providing a modern intervention that recapitulates an ancient survival mechanism.
