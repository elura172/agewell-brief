New lab research traces part of the inflammatory output of senescent cells to mitochondrial citrate metabolism, and finds that inhibiting a key transporter reduces that signaling without waking the cells.

## Three things to remember

- Inhibiting SLC25A1 reduced inflammatory signaling in cells and aged mice.
- The treatment did not cause senescent cells to resume dividing.
- Findings are preliminary; human relevance remains unknown.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern study demonstrates that inhibiting the mitochondrial citrate transporter SLC25A1 reduces the secretion of inflammatory cytokines from senescent cells, without inducing apoptosis or reversing senescence. This connects to Warburg's work by showing that mitochondrial metabolic pathways (citrate metabolism) are not just altered in cancer but also in senescence, and that these pathways can be therapeutically targeted to modulate inflammatory outputs. Furthermore, it bridges to Selye's stress concept by providing a molecular mechanism through which chronic cellular stress (senescence) translates into a systemic inflammatory state, offering a potential intervention point to mitigate the detrimental effects of inflammaging.

**Ancestral parallel:** Ancestral lifestyles, characterized by periodic fasting and physical activity, are known to enhance mitochondrial function and reduce chronic inflammation. The study's finding that mitochondrial citrate metabolism drives SASP suggests that interventions that improve mitochondrial health, such as caloric restriction or exercise, may reduce the inflammatory burden of senescent cells. This aligns with traditional practices like intermittent fasting, which has been shown to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and autophagy, potentially clearing senescent cells and reducing their inflammatory output. Thus, the mechanistic link between mitochondrial metabolism and SASP provides a molecular rationale for the anti-inflammatory benefits of ancestral lifestyle patterns.
