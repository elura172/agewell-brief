Chronological aging locks immune cells into a permanent arrest state, releasing inflammatory signals that accelerate brain tissue destruction.

As chronological age advances, patients with relapsing multiple sclerosis frequently cross a clinical threshold into progressive disease, a devastating phase defined by compartmentalized inflammation inside the central nervous system and rapid neurodegeneration. Current disease-modifying treatments manage peripheral immune attacks, yet they fail to halt this progressive decline. Reviewing the intersection of immunology and aging, researchers point to cellular senescence as a primary mechanical driver of this transition. Cells entering senescence halt their division cycles while remaining metabolically active, churning out a toxic cocktail of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, or SASP. This chemical output establishes a feedback loop that degrades surrounding neural architecture, while persistent viral infections such as Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex-1 further exhaust the immune system into immunosenescence.

The accumulation of these arrested cells directly correlates with progressive brain volume loss and treatment resistance, as aging T- and B-lymphocytes alter the microenvironment of the central nervous system. Emerging senotherapeutic interventions seek to clear these senescent cells or neutralize their secretory phenotype to mitigate neurodegeneration. However, establishing exact causal chains remains difficult in human pathology, leaving open the central question of whether senescence actively drives progressive multiple sclerosis or merely accompanies its inexorable advance.

## Key takeaways

- Chronological aging strongly predicts the clinical conversion from relapsing multiple sclerosis to the progressive neurodegenerative phase.
- Senescent cells undergo permanent cell cycle arrest and secrete a destructive mixture of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors.
- Persistent viral infections like Epstein-Barr and cytomegalovirus accelerate T- and B-cell immunosenescence within the central nervous system.
