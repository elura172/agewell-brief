Phase III trials fail because standard models miss complex human genetics and aging. New induced pluripotent stem cell techniques aim to rebuild patient-specific cell biology.

Most neurodegenerative disorders arise from complex polygenic risk landscapes intersecting with environmental exposures and aging, defying single-gene explanations. This genetic architecture helps explain why Phase III clinical trials for brain disorders fail more than 95 percent of the time, as traditional preclinical models routinely miss the human cellular context. To bridge this gap, researchers turn to patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells that capture complete genetic instructions. Yet standard reprogramming resets cellular clocks, wiping clean the epigenetic signatures of aging required for late-onset sporadic disease to manifest.

Solving this temporal bottleneck demands a dual approach spanning engineered culture platforms and direct cellular conversion. While two-dimensional monocultures and complex three-dimensional organoids reveal distinct convergent pathways and sporadic disease dynamics at cohort scale, preserving native epigenetic age remains vital for unmasking age-dependent structural failure. Direct conversion strategies bypass embryonic-like states entirely, locking in donor age signatures to grant investigators direct access to the physical phenotypes of neurodegeneration. This framework matches specific biological questions to the appropriate model system, though balancing experimental tractability against physiological fidelity continues to test the limits of in vitro biology.

## Key takeaways

- Polygenic risk variants interact with aging and environmental factors rather than single causative mutations in most neurodegeneration.
- Induced pluripotent stem cells preserve complete patient genetic architecture while faithfully recapitulating human cellular biology.
- Cellular reprogramming erases donor epigenetic aging signatures, requiring direct conversion methods to study age-dependent phenotypes.
