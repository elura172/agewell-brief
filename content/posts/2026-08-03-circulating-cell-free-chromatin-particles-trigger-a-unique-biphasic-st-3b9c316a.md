Cell-free chromatin particles from dying cells enter immune cells and activate STING in a biphasic pattern, linking cell death to DNA damage and sterile inflammation—a mechanism with deep roots in the biology of stress and adaptation.



## Three things to remember

- Circulating cfChPs enter immune cells within minutes.
- Early STING nuclear trafficking precedes DNA damage; later phosphorylation drives inflammation.
- Blocking STING halts both waves, suggesting a therapeutic target.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The preprint's mechanism: cfChPs from dying cells are internalized by immune cells, where they activate STING in a biphasic manner. Early on, STING translocates to the nucleus, triggering DNA damage responses; later, STING phosphorylation drives inflammatory cytokine production. This bridges historical work by showing that extracellular chromatin (a form of 'danger signal') is sensed via STING, a pathway that evolved to detect cytosolic DNA (a pathogen or damage signal). This connects to Popp's biophotons by suggesting that chromatin fragments carry information that is rapidly processed, and to Selye's stress response by demonstrating a cellular stress adaptation that becomes maladaptive if sustained. The STING pathway is a key integrator of cellular stress, linking DNA damage to inflammation, which is a central theme in aging and age-related diseases.

**Ancestral parallel:** Ancestral lifestyle parallels: In traditional hunter-gatherer societies, intermittent stressors (e.g., fasting, physical exertion, pathogen exposure) induced acute, resolvable inflammatory responses that promoted tissue repair and immune memory. The biphasic STING response to cfChPs may reflect an ancestral mechanism for clearing dead cells and maintaining tissue homeostasis. However, modern sedentary lifestyles with chronic low-grade cell death (e.g., from metabolic stress) may lead to persistent cfChP release, driving chronic STING activation and sterile inflammation—a maladaptive version of the ancestral stress response. Practices like intermittent fasting or regular exercise may reduce cfChP burden by enhancing autophagy and mitochondrial health, thereby mitigating the chronic phase of STING activation. This aligns with the concept of hormesis, where low-dose stressors (like cfChPs) can be beneficial, but chronic exposure becomes harmful.
