## Brain's Immune System Changes at Midlife, NIH Study Finds

New research suggests a major shift in brain immunity around middle age, which may be linked to cognitive decline and dementia risk.

### Three things to know

- NIH-supported findings reveal a new aspect of human brain aging.
- Midlife immune changes may contribute to age-related cognitive decline.
- This discovery could help understand dementia risk in older adults.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The NIH study identifies a specific time point (midlife) when microglia, the brain's immune cells, transition from a homeostatic to a more inflammatory state. This is consistent with the 'inflammaging' hypothesis, which posits that chronic low-grade inflammation accelerates aging. Mechanistically, this shift may involve changes in cytokine profiles, increased oxidative stress, and altered phagocytic activity, all of which are influenced by the HPA axis and stress hormones as described by Selye. The study's findings provide a molecular and cellular basis for the long-observed clinical correlation between midlife stress and later cognitive decline.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional lifestyle practices such as intermittent fasting, regular physical activity, and stress-reduction techniques (e.g., meditation) have been shown to reduce inflammation and support immune homeostasis. These practices may mitigate the midlife immune shift by modulating the HPA axis and reducing oxidative stress, thereby preserving cognitive function. For example, caloric restriction activates sirtuins and autophagy, which are known to dampen microglial activation. Thus, ancestral patterns of eating and activity may serve as protective factors against the midlife immune overhaul described in the study.
