When proteostasis stress forces nuclear protein Acinus into the cytoplasm, it bypasses mTOR to ignite a distinct autophagic pathway dependent on Rab32 and Lrrk.

Cellular survival during aging and proteostasis stress relies on autophagy to clear damaged components, yet the signaling logic matching specific stressors to autophagic output remains incompletely mapped. Researchers investigated Acinus, a predominantly nuclear protein long known as a splicing subunit within the ASAP complex. By engineering mutations that disrupt ASAP binding or shift Acinus into the cytoplasm, the team revealed that cytoplasmic Acinus induces autophagic flux independently of its splicing duties. Proximity labeling of the intracellular space identified the small GTPase Rab32 as a primary physical associate of cytoplasmic Acinus. Subsequent genetic dissection demonstrated that both Rab32 and its downstream effector Lrrk kinase are strictly required for this newly uncovered stress response, separating it from the classic mTor-regulated autophagy pathway.

Because human orthologs of Rab32 and Lrrk are established genetic risk factors for Parkinson's disease, this mechanistic bridge connects proteotoxic stress handling directly to neurodegenerative pathways. However, this basic science study maps these protein interactions within cellular models and does not yet demonstrate whether manipulating the Acinus-Rab32-Lrrk axis alters disease progression in human neural tissue.

## Key takeaways

- Acinus shifts from the nucleus to the cytoplasm during proteostasis stress to directly drive cellular autophagy.
- Cytoplasmic Acinus partners with the Rab32 GTPase and Lrrk kinase to trigger a specialized autophagic pathway.
- The genetic link between Acinus signaling, Rab32, and Lrrk offers new mechanistic clues for Parkinson's disease research.
