New research in mice suggests that the brain's own immune cells, not the amyloid plaques themselves, may be the primary drivers of sleep disruption in Alzheimer's disease. Depleting these cells restored more than two hours of sleep per day, offering a potential new target for therapy.

## Why this matters

Sleep problems are a common and distressing symptom of Alzheimer's disease, affecting up to 45% of patients. Poor sleep can worsen memory and thinking, and may even accelerate the disease process. Until now, scientists believed that the sticky amyloid plaques that build up in the brain were the main culprits behind this sleep loss.

But this new study, conducted in mice, suggests a different story. The real troublemakers may be microglia—the brain's resident immune cells—which launch a widespread inflammatory response to the plaques. This 'whole house' reaction, rather than the plaques themselves, appears to keep the brain from getting the deep, restorative sleep it needs.

The study's findings are particularly important because sleep disruption often begins early in Alzheimer's, sometimes before memory loss is noticeable. If microglia are indeed the cause, then targeting them could offer a way to improve sleep and potentially slow the disease's progression. This would be a paradigm shift in how we understand and treat Alzheimer's-related sleep problems.

## What the study found

Researchers at the University of Kentucky studied mice genetically engineered to develop Alzheimer's-like plaques, comparing them to normal aging mice. They monitored brain activity using EEG and EMG recordings at two time points: when plaques first appeared (6 months) and when they were extensive (18 months).

Surprisingly, sleep disruption was just as severe at 6 months as at 18 months, even though plaque burden more than doubled. This 'ceiling effect' suggested that the initial immune response, not the accumulating plaques, was driving the problem. When the researchers used a drug to temporarily eliminate about 87% of microglia, the mice gained more than two hours of sleep per day, with longer periods of deep NREM sleep—all without changing plaque levels.

The study also distinguished the effects of normal aging from Alzheimer's pathology. Normal aging primarily reduced REM sleep, while amyloid pathology selectively reduced NREM sleep, the stage crucial for physical repair and memory consolidation. This suggests that Alzheimer's specifically targets the most restorative sleep, which could explain why patients feel unrefreshed even after a full night's rest.

## How to interpret the results

This is an animal study, and the findings may not directly apply to humans. The drug used, Pexidartinib, is not approved for Alzheimer's and is not safe for long-term use. Depleting microglia entirely could have serious consequences, as these cells also help clear debris and fight infection.

The study does, however, point to a new avenue for treatment: calming microglia rather than eliminating them. The researchers are already testing existing drugs, like metformin and stiripentol, that might reduce microglial overactivity. They also identified distinct EEG patterns that could someday help diagnose Alzheimer's earlier, using portable devices at home.

It's important to note that the study does not prove that inflammation causes sleep loss in humans, nor does it show that restoring sleep will slow Alzheimer's progression. The results are promising but preliminary, and much more research is needed before any clinical recommendations can be made.

## Practical next steps

For now, there is no immediate clinical application. But the findings underscore the importance of sleep for brain health. If you or a loved one are experiencing sleep problems, talk to a doctor. Good sleep hygiene—regular sleep schedule, avoiding caffeine late in the day, and creating a restful environment—can help.

The researchers hope that by targeting microglia, they can restore restorative sleep and potentially slow the progression of Alzheimer's. While that is still years away, this study brings us a step closer to understanding and treating one of the most challenging symptoms of the disease.

In the meantime, staying physically active, maintaining social connections, and managing chronic conditions like diabetes and high blood pressure are all strategies that may support brain health and improve sleep quality. Always consult a healthcare provider before making significant changes to your health routine.

## Three things to remember

- Microglia, not plaques, drive sleep loss in Alzheimer's mice.
- Depleting microglia restored over two hours of sleep daily.
- Findings are preliminary; human studies are needed.
