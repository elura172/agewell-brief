Senescent cells accumulate in inflamed arteries, driving tissue damage through secretory profiles that current anti-inflammatory drugs leave unaddressed.

Giant cell arteritis strikes primarily in older adults, transforming large blood vessels into sites of relentless immune assault and structural decay. When vascular dendritic cells, T cells, and monocytes infiltrate the vessel wall, resident smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells frequently slip into cellular senescence. Rather than quietly exiting, these stalled cells adopt a secretory phenotype, flooding the local microenvironment with pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix-degrading enzymes. This biochemical barrage continually feeds the inflammatory cascade and drives abnormal tissue remodeling, explaining why standard therapies that merely suppress acute inflammation often fail to halt persistent vascular injury.

Intervening directly in this aging-associated cellular biology requires compounds capable of either clearing senescent cells or quieting their output. Candidate senolytics like dasatinib, fisetin, quercetin, and curcumin trigger apoptotic pathways within the damaged cell populations, while senomorphics such as rapamycin, metformin, ruxolitinib, and resveratrol dampen signaling nodes including mTOR, NF-kappaB, and JAK/STAT pathways. Because this analysis draws from a theoretical review rather than clinical trial data, optimizing drug selection, dosing schedules, and long-term vascular safety remains an open challenge for future translational research.

## Key takeaways

- Senescent vascular and immune cells accumulate within affected arteries, perpetuating chronic tissue damage through pro-inflammatory secretory profiles.
- Senolytic compounds aim to induce apoptosis in damaged cells, while senomorphics suppress destructive secretory phenotypes without cell clearance.
- Candidate agents modulate core molecular pathways like mTOR, NF-kappaB, and JAK/STAT to potentially restore vascular immune balance.
