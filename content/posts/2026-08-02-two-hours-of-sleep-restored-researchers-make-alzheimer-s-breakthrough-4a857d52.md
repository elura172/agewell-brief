## Two hours of sleep restored: Researchers make Alzheimer's breakthrough

New research suggests that lack of sleep might cause a harmful immune response in the brain, offering a new target for treatment.

### Three things to know

- Sleep loss may trigger an automatic brain response that damages healthy tissue.
- The response is like a sprinkler flooding a house after a small fire.
- Researchers believe this discovery could lead to new Alzheimer's treatments.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern preprint demonstrates that sleep loss triggers an immune response in the brain that damages healthy tissue, akin to an overactive 'sprinkler system' after a small fire. This aligns with Selye's GAS: sleep deprivation acts as a chronic stressor, leading to an exaggerated inflammatory response (alarm phase) that, if prolonged, causes collateral damage (exhaustion phase). The immune activation involves microglia and complement pathways, which are metabolically demanding and may shift brain energy metabolism toward glycolysis, reminiscent of Warburg's findings. This metabolic shift could exacerbate neuronal damage, providing a mechanistic link between sleep loss, immune response, and Alzheimer's pathology.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles, such as those of the Hadza or San people, often involve polyphasic sleep patterns and variable sleep durations tied to environmental cues (e.g., temperature, daylight). These patterns may reduce chronic sleep deprivation and associated immune overactivation. Physical mechanisms include the circadian regulation of immune cells (e.g., natural killer cells) and the glymphatic system's clearance of metabolic waste during deep sleep, which is enhanced by regular sleep-wake cycles. Ancestral diets rich in omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants may also modulate neuroinflammation, offering a protective parallel to modern sleep loss-induced damage.
