Middle-aged adults with a parental history of dementia display a distinct immune shift toward memory T cells rather than generalized neural aging.

Investigating how familial risk translates into cellular wear, researchers examined DNA methylation, telomere length, immune cell composition, and magnetic resonance imaging brain scans in 1,204 middle-aged adults. Parental dementia history did not accelerate generalized epigenetic aging or structural brain aging. Instead, it triggered a marked expansion of CD8 memory T cells alongside nominal shifts in CD4 memory T cells and memory B cells. Accounting for these expanded CD8 memory cells fully accounted for the observed shortening of telomeres, pointing to a targeted, immune-specific cellular footprint rather than a whole-body aging trajectory.

At the same time, earlier parental onset and poorer parental cognitive performance tracked with faster biological pacing, lower naive immune populations, and larger brain age gaps. These findings suggest that familial dementia risk operates through distinct physiological channels that reshape peripheral immunity long before clinical symptoms emerge. Yet because this work relies on observational associations in midlife cohorts, the exact signaling pathways linking lineage-linked cognitive decline to lymphocyte remodeling remain to be experimentally proven.

## Key takeaways

- Parental dementia history expands CD8 memory T cells without accelerating brain or epigenetic aging.
- Shorter telomere length in at-risk offspring stems specifically from expanded CD8 memory T cell populations.
- Poorer parental cognitive performance correlates with lower naive immune proportions and larger brain age gaps.
