Multiomic profiling reveals that farmed salmon accumulate cellular fats through conserved mammalian aging pathways, while dietary spermidine clears the blockage.

## An evolutionary bridge in metabolic aging

Age-related metabolic decline in mammals stems from a breakdown in how tissues partition and burn energy. Over time, liver and muscle cells lose their ability to balance de novo fat synthesis against mitochondrial oxidation, trapping unmanaged lipid droplets in the cytoplasm. This failure destroys metabolic flexibility, stripping cells of the capacity to shift fuel sources under nutritional load or thermal stress.

A central question in evolutionary biology is whether this progressive energetic decay is an exclusive feature of terrestrial endotherms or a universal property of vertebrate physiology. Farmed Atlantic salmon offer a transparent window onto this transition. As these fish cycle through intensive aquaculture production, their metabolic organs endure physiological strain that mirrors the cellular stress signatures of aging mammalian tissues.

## Mapping the multiomic landscape of salmon metabolism

To map these metabolic transitions, researchers applied high-throughput multiomics to farmed salmon across distinct life stages. By integrating global transcriptomic and lipidomic profiles, the investigators tracked how gene expression and specific lipid species shift over time in key metabolic organs, benchmarking these trajectories against established mammalian aging profiles.

The multiomic signature exposed a production-induced aging profile defined by stalled lipid flux. As the fish matured, their tissue profiles revealed a systemic collapse in lipid clearance pathways, driving intracellular fat accumulation that closely resembles hepatic steatosis and metabolic syndrome in aging mammals.

## Polyamine signaling restores intracellular lipid handling

To clear this metabolic bottleneck, the researchers introduced dietary spermidine, a natural polyamine known to rewire cellular maintenance networks. In early model organisms, spermidine was shown by Frank Madeo in 2009 to stimulate macroautophagy and sweep away cellular debris, establishing polyamines as core regulators of metabolic health.

In the salmon model, dietary spermidine supplementation supercharged lipid processing in metabolic tissues. By bolstering cellular lipid transport and enzymatic flux, the compound forced cells to burn off excess internal fats, preventing the destructive lipid engorgement that typically ruins high-density production cohorts.

## Comparative longevity and evidentiary limits

These findings prove that metabolic aging pathways are conserved across vast evolutionary distances, linking teleost fish physiology directly to mammalian longevity biology. Demonstrating that polyamines rescue lipid handling in aquatic species expands our understanding of how fundamental metabolic maintenance engines operate across divergent vertebrate classes.

However, strict translational boundaries remain. While the study confirms that spermidine improves lipid processing in farmed fish, these results cannot be directly extrapolated to human dietary interventions or age-related metabolic therapies without dedicated clinical trials.

## Key takeaways

- Multiomic maps show aging farmed salmon accumulate cellular fats through mechanisms mirroring mammalian lipid dysregulation.
- Dietary spermidine ramps up tissue lipid clearance and halts destructive intracellular fat accumulation in production salmon.
- The data prove that polyamine-driven metabolic maintenance circuits are deeply conserved across widely divergent vertebrate lineages.
