Declining muscle performance reflects a systemic breakdown in oxygen delivery, neural signaling, and mitochondrial energy production rather than isolated tissue decay.

For decades, medicine has treated sarcopenia as a local failure of skeletal muscle tissue, focusing clinical efforts on preserving muscle mass. Yet epidemiological data reveal that functional strength predicts disability and mortality far more tightly than total muscle mass, while interventions that successfully build mass frequently fail to restore physical performance. To resolve this paradox, researchers propose that age-related muscle decline is an emergent property of systemic energetic failure. In this framework, physical capability depends on the precise, synchronized coordination of neural activation, vascular delivery of oxygen and metabolic substrates, and the intracellular transformation of those inputs into adenosine triphosphate by mitochondria. As organisms age, the physiological communication across these distributed systems breaks down. The resulting energetic deficit manifests clinically not merely as thinner muscle fibers, but as excessive physical fatigability, sluggish post-exertion recovery, and progressive mobility disability.

This integrated physiological perspective shifts the diagnostic and therapeutic targets for maintaining late-life functional independence. Rather than measuring muscle cross-sectional area alone, clinicians must evaluate multisystem coordination and recovery dynamics to capture early signs of functional collapse before irreversible disability sets in. Therapeutics designed exclusively to rebuild muscle tissue will continually fall short if they fail to restore the upstream vascular, neural, and metabolic networks that fuel physical action. However, this reframing remains a high-level physiological hypothesis that synthesizes existing epidemiological and mechanistic clues rather than a definitively proven causal model.

## Key takeaways

- Sarcopenia arises when aging disrupts the coordination among neural activation, substrate delivery, and mitochondrial energy production.
- Muscle strength tracks disability and mortality more closely than muscle mass, and building mass fails to restore function.
- Early clinical indicators of systemic energetic failure include heightened physical fatigability and impaired post-exertion recovery capacity.
