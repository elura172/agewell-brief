By altering bacterial pyruvate metabolism, dietary sugar shifts host lifespan and development in C. elegans through a single sphingomyelin enzyme.

Genetically identical organisms often exhibit radically different physiological responses to the same nutrients, a variability long suspected to originate in the gut microbiome. To isolate the physical mechanics of this three-way interaction between diet, microbes, and host, researchers tracked the longevity and developmental arrest of C. elegans carrying mutations in daf-2, the nematode insulin-like receptor. When fed standard Escherichia coli OP50, dietary sugar suppressed the extended lifespan and alternative dauer larval states typical of daf-2 mutants, a suppression that failed to occur when the worms consumed alternative bacterial strains. Transposon mutagenesis screens revealed that this dietary sugar effect relies entirely on bacterial pyruvate dehydrogenase activity. When bacterial pyruvate dehydrogenase is impaired, pyruvate and lactate pool in the local environment, driving up the expression of asm-3, a host gene encoding acid sphingomyelinase. Genetic deletion of asm-3 completely abolishes the phenotypic shift, proving that the enzyme acts as an obligate molecular bridge between microbial metabolism and host insulin signaling.

These findings uncover a direct biochemical route by which microbial metabolic output dictates host physiological destiny under altered nutrient conditions. Rather than acting as passive digests, bacterial metabolic intermediates directly command host gene transcription to reshape fundamental aging pathways. However, these mechanistic insights remain strictly confined to the nematode worm and defined bacterial strains, leaving the question of whether identical biochemical relays operate across mammalian tissue architectures entirely unresolved.

## Key takeaways

- Dietary sugar suppresses longevity and developmental programs in insulin-mutant nematodes fed specific bacterial strains.
- Bacterial pyruvate dehydrogenase activity controls whether sugar availability alters host insulin signaling pathways.
- Accumulated bacterial pyruvate and lactate induce host expression of the acid sphingomyelinase gene asm-3.
