Some products marketed as dietary supplements or foods contain undeclared drugs. The FDA's searchable list can help you check.

## Three things to remember

- Hidden drug ingredients can appear in 'all natural' products.
- The FDA maintains a searchable notification list online.
- Check specific products before you buy or use them.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern FDA notification list operationalizes the biophysical principle that drug molecules interact with specific receptors and ion channels (e.g., PDE5 inhibition by sildenafil, androgen receptor activation by steroids) to produce measurable physiological changes. Hidden ingredients bypass standard pharmacokinetic and toxicological screening, yet their mechanisms remain identical to those of approved drugs. Thus, the FDA's list serves as a real-world validation of Guyton's systems-level view: even small, undeclared doses can perturb homeostatic set points (e.g., blood pressure, hormonal balance), and Kelsey's legacy that regulatory oversight must extend to all sources of drug exposure, including supplements.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional societies relied on whole-plant preparations (e.g., willow bark for pain, coca leaf for energy) where active compounds were present in low, naturally buffered concentrations, and knowledge of toxicity was passed down through generations. The modern problem of hidden synthetic drugs in 'natural' supplements breaks this ancestral safety mechanism: concentrated, isolated actives (e.g., sildenafil, anabolic steroids) lack the natural co-factors and dose-limiting plant matrices that historically provided a built-in safety margin. This underscores that ancestral practices were not 'drug-free' but rather relied on identifiable, whole-organism pharmacology—whereas today's undisclosed additives bypass both traditional knowledge and modern regulation, increasing risk of adverse effects.
