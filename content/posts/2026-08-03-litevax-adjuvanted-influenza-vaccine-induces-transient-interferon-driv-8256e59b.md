A study finds an experimental adjuvant may help rebalance immune responses in people over 60, improving early antibody quality without changing overall levels.

## Why it matters

As we age, our immune system changes in ways that can weaken vaccine responses—a process called immunosenescence. This is one reason older adults are at higher risk for severe flu, and why standard flu shots may not work as well in later life.

Adjuvants are ingredients added to vaccines to boost the immune response. The new LiteVax Adjuvant (LVA) is designed to do this more effectively in older adults. If it works, it could lead to flu vaccines that offer better protection for people over 60.

## What the study found

In a small human study, 56 adults (18–45 and 60+) received a standard quadrivalent flu vaccine with or without LVA. Researchers tracked immune responses over time using advanced techniques like single-cell gene expression and antibody profiling.

LVA triggered a rapid, short-lived interferon-driven response in monocytes—a type of white blood cell—and enhanced interactions between dendritic cells and T cells. It also shifted flu-specific CD4+ T cells toward a memory phenotype and accelerated early IgG3 antibody responses, which are important for viral neutralization.

Notably, LVA increased IgG-producing plasmablasts without raising overall antibody levels. The authors say it reduced age-related differences in early immune responses, suggesting it may help close the gap between younger and older adults.

## How to interpret this

This is a preprint, meaning it has not yet been peer-reviewed. The study is small, and the findings are mechanistic—they show how LVA affects immune markers, not whether it prevents flu or reduces severe illness.

The results are promising but preliminary. The authors note that LVA did not change overall antibody magnitude, so its benefit may be in improving response quality rather than quantity. Whether this translates into better clinical protection remains unknown.

It's also important to remember that this is one study. More research, including larger trials and real-world effectiveness studies, is needed before any conclusions can be drawn about LVA's value in flu vaccination.

## What this means for you

For now, the practical takeaway is to continue following current flu vaccine recommendations. Standard flu shots remain the best available protection, even if they are less effective in older adults.

If you have questions about flu vaccination, talk to your healthcare provider. They can help you understand your options, including high-dose or adjuvanted vaccines that are already approved for older adults.

Keep an eye on future research about LVA and other adjuvants. This study is a step toward potentially better flu vaccines for people over 60, but it's not a reason to change your behavior today.

## Three things to remember

- LVA is an experimental adjuvant added to flu vaccines.
- Study found it improved early immune response quality.
- More research is needed before it becomes available.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The LiteVax Adjuvant (LVA) appears to enhance early antibody quality by modulating the innate immune system, possibly through pattern recognition receptors (e.g., TLR agonists) that activate dendritic cells and promote germinal center reactions. This aligns with the historical understanding that adjuvants like alum (used since the 1920s) primarily boost antibody quantity, whereas newer adjuvants (e.g., MF59, AS04) can improve antibody affinity maturation. The study's focus on early antibody quality in over-60s directly addresses the age-related decline in B-cell repertoire diversity and somatic hypermutation, as described by Fudenberg and later confirmed by studies on aging and vaccine responses. By rebalancing the immune response, LVA may counteract the chronic low-grade inflammation ('inflammaging') that impairs vaccine efficacy in older adults, a concept rooted in the work of Franceschi (2000) on inflammaging.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional practices such as intermittent fasting and caloric restriction have been shown to enhance immune function and reduce inflammation, potentially improving vaccine responses in older adults. Mechanistically, these practices activate autophagy and sirtuin pathways, which are known to modulate immune cell metabolism and function. This parallels the goal of LVA to rebalance immune responses, suggesting that lifestyle interventions that mimic the metabolic state of youth could complement adjuvant strategies to improve vaccine efficacy in the elderly.
