A vast genomic map reveals that Alzheimer's risk variants cluster heavily in circulating immune cells, liver tissue, and gut linings.

To determine whether Alzheimer's disease originates strictly within the central nervous system, researchers systematically partitioned polygenic risk across 166 tissue annotations and 4.4 million individual cells from 28 peripheral organs and 100 brain regions. Rather than isolating risk solely within brain-resident microglia, the genomic data demonstrated robust enrichment across peripheral immune, barrier, and metabolic tissues. The strongest signals concentrated in circulating and tissue-resident myeloid cells, liver hepatocytes, cardiac muscle cells, and intestinal epithelial cells. Interestingly, while metabolic risk enrichment predominantly mapped to the well-known APOE locus, the peripheral immune signals operated entirely independently of APOE, pointing toward distinct peripheral regulatory cascades.

These findings broaden the conceptual map of neurodegenerative risk, suggesting that non-neuronal organs actively participate in systemic resilience or vulnerability long before clinical symptoms appear. Mendelian randomization further indicated that many of these peripheral immune-gene variants exert protective effects, potentially serving as defensive buffers against systemic inflammation. However, computational co-localization and genetic mapping alone cannot prove causal disease mechanics. Experimental validation remains necessary to establish how these distant cellular programs physically interact with the blood-brain barrier and central nervous system over a human lifespan.

## Key takeaways

- Alzheimer's genetic risk is heavily enriched in peripheral immune, metabolic, and epithelial barrier tissues.
- Immune cell genetic risk operates independently of APOE, while metabolic risk heavily depends on the APOE locus.
- Many peripheral immune-gene signals appear to exert protective effects rather than driving tissue damage.
