Long flights disrupt more than sleep—your gut bacteria may also feel the time change, leading to fatigue and digestive issues.

## Three things to remember

- Jet lag affects your gut microbiome, not just your sleep.
- Gut disruption can cause fatigue and digestive discomfort.
- Plan ahead to help your gut adjust to new time zones.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern finding that long flights shift the gut microbiome extends the historical work by showing that circadian disruption (as characterized by Rosbash et al.) directly alters the composition and rhythmicity of gut microbiota, which in turn influences host metabolism and immune function via the gut-brain axis (Cryan and Dinan). The microbiome's own circadian oscillations are synchronized by host clock genes and feeding cues; when these are desynchronized by rapid time-zone changes, the microbial community shifts, leading to dysbiosis that manifests as fatigue and digestive discomfort. This validates the gut-brain-axis model by demonstrating a concrete, travel-induced perturbation that links circadian biology to microbial ecology and systemic symptoms.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer and agrarian lifestyles were tightly coupled to natural light-dark cycles and seasonal food availability, which likely maintained stable gut microbial rhythms. Practices such as time-restricted eating (eating only during daylight hours) and consuming fermented foods rich in probiotics and prebiotics (e.g., kimchi, kefir, and cultured vegetables) would have supported microbial resilience against circadian disruptions. The physical mechanism is that these practices reinforce the host's circadian clock and provide substrates for beneficial bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, which help regulate intestinal barrier integrity and immune function, thereby mitigating the effects of travel-induced jet lag on the gut.
