MIT researchers discovered that deep-sleep brain waves and vascular responses weaken with age, reducing the nocturnal fluid movements that clear cellular waste from the brain.

During deep non-REM sleep, the human brain generates slow electrical oscillations that drive rhythmic surges of cerebrospinal fluid through perivascular spaces to flush out accumulated metabolic waste. Researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology sought to determine whether this nocturnal plumbing system degrades with age, recruiting younger adults aged 18 to 39 and older participants aged 60 to 84 for simultaneous functional MRI, specialized fluid-flow MRI, and electroencephalography monitoring. When the participants fell asleep inside the scanners, the data revealed a stark divergence. While younger adults produced powerful slow-wave oscillations accompanied by massive cerebrospinal fluid surges, older participants exhibited substantially smaller fluid waves. To uncover the biophysical drivers of this decline, the team measured neurovascular coupling and vascular reactivity by having awake participants watch flickering visual patterns and hold their breath to elevate arterial carbon dioxide. Older adults showed blunted vascular responses, indicating that stiffened or sluggish blood vessel dynamics limit the volumetric shifts required to power waste-clearing fluid waves.

These physical impairments tracked with structural markers, as brain scans showed that older participants with greater gray matter atrophy experienced the steepest drops in cerebrospinal fluid movement. However, vascular sluggishness did not account for the entirety of the decline, pointing to additional unmeasured biological changes accompanying human aging. Crucially, the observational cross-sectional design cannot establish whether weakened fluid flow directly causes brain tissue loss or subsequent dementia. Yet the discovery that a subset of older adults retained youthful, robust cleaning waves opens a critical investigative window into the physiological factors that preserve neurological maintenance across the lifespan.

## Key takeaways

- Cerebrospinal fluid surges that flush metabolic waste during deep sleep are significantly smaller in older adults.
- Aged blood vessels show blunted dilation responses to neural activity and carbon dioxide, constraining fluid movement.
- Some older adults retain youthful fluid waves, suggesting specific biological factors can preserve nocturnal brain cleansing.
