A new analysis by Originality.ai suggests most herbal remedy books on Amazon may be AI-generated, raising concerns about quality and accuracy.
## Three things to remember

- Originality.ai scanned 558 herbal remedy titles on Amazon.
- Found 82% likely written by AI, from January to September.
- Examples include ginkgo tinctures and citrus-immune gummies.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern finding that 82% of herbal remedy books on Amazon are likely AI-written connects to historical antecedents through the persistent issue of unverified and potentially harmful botanical information. Historically, books like those by Gerard and Culpeper were compiled without rigorous pharmacological testing, leading to misidentifications and toxic dosages. Today, AI-generated texts similarly lack peer review and mechanistic validation, but they scale the problem: they can produce thousands of plausible-sounding but factually unreliable guides, increasing the risk of adverse herb-drug interactions and incorrect dosing. The bridge is the absence of a physical mechanism—neither historical nor AI authors performed controlled assays or clinical trials—so the information is not grounded in biophysical evidence, making it dangerous for consumers who rely on it for health decisions.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional herbal knowledge was typically transmitted orally within communities, with empirical testing over generations and direct observation of effects—a form of 'trial-and-error' that, while not randomized, was grounded in repeated physical experience. In contrast, AI-generated books lack this experiential validation; they synthesize patterns from text without any sensory or physiological feedback. Ancestral practices also involved local sourcing and seasonal harvesting, ensuring freshness and correct plant identification, whereas AI texts may recommend generic or misidentified species. The physical mechanism behind ancestral reliability was the direct dose-response relationship observed in individuals, which AI cannot replicate, highlighting the need for modern consumers to seek evidence-based sources or consult trained herbalists.
