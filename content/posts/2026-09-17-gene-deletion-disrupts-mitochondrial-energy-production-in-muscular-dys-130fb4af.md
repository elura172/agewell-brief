Researchers trace a genetic driver of muscular dystrophy to broken mitochondrial dynamics, showing that chemical activators can restore cellular energy production.

Dystroglycanopathies are severe muscular dystrophies triggered by faulty sugar-tagging of the structural protein alpha-dystroglycan. To understand why patients also suffer profound mitochondrial decay in their muscle biopsies, scientists engineered a mouse model lacking the causative gene Crppa. These knockout mice suffered early neonatal death, muscle weakness, cerebellar underdevelopment, and striking structural deformations inside their skeletal muscle mitochondria. Transcriptomic profiling exposed a silenced cyclic AMP and protein kinase A signaling axis, which left downstream dynamin-related protein 1 starved of its regulatory phosphorylation site at serine 637. Because this molecular cascade controls how mitochondria fuse and divide to maintain energy output, the breakdown left cells severely depleted of ATP.

When investigators tested whether this energy failure extended beyond whole animals, they observed the identical biochemical defect in cultured mouse myoblasts and human patient fibroblasts bearing CRPPA mutations. Dosing these cellular models with chemical activators of the cyclic AMP pathway successfully restored protein kinase A activity, rescued dynamin-related protein 1 phosphorylation, revived mitochondrial membrane potential, and lifted ATP production back toward normal levels. While these experiments map a clear molecular link between structural gene defects and cellular power failure, the findings currently rely entirely on animal and cellular models, leaving the translation to human therapeutic interventions to future clinical investigation.

## Key takeaways

- Crppa knockout mice displayed early neonatal lethality alongside severe muscle weakness and structural mitochondrial defects.
- RNA sequencing revealed that CRPPA deficiency shuts down the cyclic AMP pathway and starves dynamin-related protein 1 of phosphorylation.
- Treating mutant cellular models with pathway activators restored both mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production.
