A four-year Lund University study shows smoking, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease accelerate white matter damage, a key driver of vascular dementia. The findings suggest that addressing these risks could delay cognitive decline by reducing multiple brain insults simultaneously.

## Why this matters

Dementia is not a single disease but a spectrum of brain pathologies. The two most common causes—Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia—often coexist, complicating prevention and treatment. While age and genetics are fixed, nearly half of dementia cases may be linked to modifiable risks like smoking, high blood pressure, and heart disease. This means a substantial portion of cognitive decline could be delayed by targeting vascular and metabolic health.

The Lund University study, published in The Journal of Prevention of Alzheimer's Disease, tracked 500 cognitively healthy adults (average age 65) for four years. It measured changes in white matter—nerve fibers critical for brain connectivity—and levels of amyloid-β and tau proteins, the hallmarks of Alzheimer's. The results provide a mechanistic link between lifestyle factors and brain damage, offering a tangible target for intervention.

## What was found

The study identified that most modifiable risk factors—smoking, cardiovascular disease, high blood lipids, and high blood pressure—were associated with damage to the brain's blood vessels and a faster accumulation of white matter changes. These changes impair blood vessel function, leading to vascular brain damage that can culminate in vascular dementia. This aligns with the historical understanding from Thomas Willis in 1664, who first linked cognitive decline to vascular abnormalities.

Additionally, the researchers found possible connections between diabetes and increased amyloid-β accumulation, and between lower BMI and faster tau accumulation. However, these findings are preliminary and require validation. The study's strength lies in its longitudinal design, tracking changes over four years, but its limitations include a relatively small, healthy cohort and a short duration relative to dementia's long preclinical phase.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The Lund University study's finding that smoking, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease accelerate white matter damage directly validates the vascular hypothesis of dementia proposed by Osler and Hachinski. Mechanistically, these risk factors induce endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and cerebral hypoperfusion, leading to chronic ischemia of white matter tracts. This ischemia triggers oligodendrocyte injury, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and microglial activation, resulting in demyelination and axonal loss—visible as white matter hyperintensities on MRI. The study's measurement of amyloid-β and tau levels also connects to the vascular hypothesis by showing that vascular damage can exacerbate Alzheimer's pathology, supporting the concept of mixed dementia where vascular and neurodegenerative insults synergize.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles, such as those of the Hadza or Tsimané, exhibit exceptionally low rates of hypertension, smoking, and cardiovascular disease, and correspondingly low dementia prevalence. Their diets are high in fiber and potassium, low in sodium and processed foods, and they engage in high daily physical activity, which maintains endothelial function and cerebral blood flow. This lifestyle parallels the modifiable risk factors identified in the study: by avoiding smoking, maintaining healthy blood pressure through diet and activity, and supporting cardiovascular health, ancestral populations naturally protect white matter integrity and cognitive function, offering a physiological blueprint for dementia prevention.



The study does not prove that lifestyle changes prevent dementia, but it strongly suggests that vascular risk factors accelerate white matter damage, a key component of vascular dementia. The physical mechanism is clear: hypertension and smoking cause endothelial dysfunction, leading to reduced cerebral blood flow and microvascular injury, which manifests as white matter hyperintensities. This vascular damage can coexist with Alzheimer's pathology, amplifying cognitive decline.

From a biophysical perspective, this echoes Otto Warburg's principle that cellular health depends on efficient mitochondrial respiration. Vascular damage reduces oxygen and nutrient delivery to neurons, impairing mitochondrial function and promoting oxidative stress. This can trigger tau hyperphosphorylation and amyloid aggregation, linking vascular and Alzheimer's pathologies. Thus, improving vascular health may not only prevent vascular dementia but also mitigate Alzheimer's progression.

## Practical next steps

The findings underscore the importance of managing blood pressure, quitting smoking, and controlling cardiovascular disease and high blood lipids. These actions reduce vascular damage and may delay dementia onset. While the study did not test specific interventions, the evidence supports a proactive approach to vascular health as a dementia prevention strategy.

For individuals, this means regular monitoring of blood pressure and lipid profiles, adopting a heart-healthy diet, and engaging in physical activity. The ancestral wisdom of Mediterranean and Okinawan diets—rich in vegetables, fish, and low in processed foods—aligns with these recommendations, promoting endothelial function and cerebral blood flow. As the researchers note, focusing on vascular and metabolic risk factors can help reduce the combined effects of multiple brain changes.

## Three things to remember

- Smoking, hypertension, and heart disease accelerate white matter damage.
- Vascular risks may also influence Alzheimer's protein accumulation.
- Managing these factors could delay dementia by reducing brain damage.
