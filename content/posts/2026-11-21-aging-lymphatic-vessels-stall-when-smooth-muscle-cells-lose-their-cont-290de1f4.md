Mouse collecting vessels fail during aging not because their smooth muscle cells die, but because a crucial transcription factor, SRF, quietens and strips the cells of their motor proteins.

Lymphatic drainage depends on collecting vessels wrapped in smooth muscle cells that squeeze in steady rhythms, pushing tissue fluid back toward the heart. When these vessels fail with age, fluid pools in tissues, a collapse long blamed on smooth muscle cells dying off and peeling away from the vessel wall. New research by Michael M. Orlich and colleagues reveals that aged muscle cells remain anchored in place, but shut down the genetic program that builds their molecular motors. Examining mouse ear vessels alongside single-cell sequencing data, the team caught a steep decline in serum response factor (SRF), the master transcription factor that maintains the cell's internal contractile architecture. When the authors selectively deleted Srf in mural cells, the vessels ballooned, mimicking the structural decay of aging while leaving lymphatic endothelial cells, capillary networks, and valve structures unharmed. High-speed microscopic imaging revealed that without ongoing SRF signaling, individual muscle cells stopped pulling against the vessel wall, halting the pump cycle and blocking dye drainage to downstream lymph nodes.

By showing that smooth muscle cells require active SRF to maintain their contractile state, the authors established a clear genetic driver for lymphatic aging. The work reframes vessel decay as a loss of cell identity and mechanical force rather than structural cell death. These results remain confined to rodent models and transcriptomic profiles. The study did not test targeted therapies or establish whether reactivating SRF can safely rebuild lost contractile power in aging humans.

## Key takeaways

- Aged collecting vessels dilate because smooth muscle cells stop building contractile machinery, not because the cells die or detach.
- Serum response factor (SRF) must fire continuously throughout adult life to sustain the muscle gene network that powers vessel pumping.
- Deleting Srf in mouse mural cells halts lymphatic drainage while leaving endothelial walls, capillaries, and valves physically intact.
