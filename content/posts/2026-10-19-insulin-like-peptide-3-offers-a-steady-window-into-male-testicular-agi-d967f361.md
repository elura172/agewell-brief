Unlike pulsatile testosterone, INSL3 provides a constitutive readout of Leydig cell mass, tracking life-course endocrine health across shifting physiological states.

The male hypothalamo-pituitary-gonadal axis operates as an integrated endocrine network where hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone commands pituitary gonadotropins to drive testicular steroidogenesis and spermatogenesis. Traditional biomarkers like testosterone and luteinizing hormone fluctuate wildly throughout the day due to pulsatile secretory bursts, acute stress, and environmental exposures, obscuring underlying cellular reality. A recent narrative review examines insulin-like peptide 3, a small protein secreted directly by testicular Leydig cells that bypasses this erratic secretory rhythm. Because INSL3 release scales linearly with the physical number and differentiation status of adult Leydig cells rather than responding to minute-to-minute neural pulses, it serves as a stable molecular proxy for long-term glandular capacity across fetal development, puberty, and adult aging.

Tracking this constitutive peptide grants researchers finer resolution to evaluate how environmental endocrine disruptors, childhood nutrition, and adult adiposity erode testicular reserve long before clinical hypogonadism manifests. Yet despite its promise for mapping age-related morbidity and Leydig cell senescence, the physiological boundaries governing this system remain broad. The reference range for healthy adult men spans a remarkably wide concentration gradient for reasons that are not yet understood, and the peptide reflects only the steroidogenic arm of the gonad rather than direct sperm production.

## Key takeaways

- Insulin-like peptide 3 is continuously secreted by testicular Leydig cells without the pulsatile fluctuations characteristic of testosterone.
- The peptide reflects absolute Leydig cell number and differentiation status, offering a steady proxy for long-term glandular capacity.
- Measuring INSL3 provides granularity on how environmental disruptors, childhood development, and lifestyle factors impact adult testicular health.
