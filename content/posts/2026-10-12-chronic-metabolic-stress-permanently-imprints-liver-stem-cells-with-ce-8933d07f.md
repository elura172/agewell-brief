A high-fat diet drives lasting mitochondrial failure and growth arrest in liver regenerative cells, compromising tissue repair even after removal from the metabolic stressor.

When a mammalian liver endures the chronic metabolic insult of a high-fat diet, the damage extends far beyond lipid accumulation and inflammation in mature tissue. Researchers investigating male rats exposed to this metabolic stress discovered that the organ's regenerative compartment undergoes a durable shift toward a senescence-like state. Following carbon tetrachloride injury, these animals displayed blunt proliferative responses. To test whether this memory of metabolic stress resided directly within the stem and progenitor cells, the team isolated liver organoids and cultured them outside the original high-fat environment. Through three successive passages, the organoids maintained elevated senescence-associated beta-galactosidase activity, depleted nuclear structural protein Lamin B1, and diminished replication markers like Ki67 alongside impaired serial propagation. Transcriptomic and functional profiling traced this arrest to severe mitochondrial dysfunction, marked by impaired oxygen consumption and elevated oxidative stress.

Targeting this biophysical failure point with MitoQ, a mitochondria-directed antioxidant, curbed reactive oxygen species generation, dampened inflammatory secretions, and partially rescued organoid formation efficiency. These findings demonstrate that chronic metabolic strain physically rewires the regenerative machinery of the liver, imposing a persistent senescence program that survives outside the systemic environment. However, because these conclusions derive entirely from male rat models and in vitro organoid cultures, whether human liver progenitor compartments exhibit identical durable epigenetic or mitochondrial imprinting under metabolic stress remains to be established.

## Key takeaways

- High-fat feeding prompts hepatic steatosis and locks liver progenitor cells into a persistent, growth-arrested senescence state.
- Isolated organoids retain impaired propagation capacity, high beta-galactosidase activity, and low Ki67 through three continuous passages.
- A mitochondria-targeted antioxidant reduces oxidative stress and partially restores formation efficiency in stressed regenerative organoids.
