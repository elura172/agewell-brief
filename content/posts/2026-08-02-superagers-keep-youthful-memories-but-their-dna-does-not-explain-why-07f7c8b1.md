A new study finds that people in their 80s and 90s with exceptional memory don't carry fewer Alzheimer's-related genes. The search for what protects them continues.
## Why this matters

Forgetting names or where you left your keys can feel like an inevitable part of aging. But a small group of older adults, called SuperAgers, seem to defy that trend. They reach their 80s and 90s with memory abilities that rival people decades younger. Understanding what makes them different could help all of us protect our brains as we age.

For nearly 20 years, scientists have studied SuperAgers to uncover their secrets. One obvious guess was that these individuals simply inherited fewer genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease. If true, a simple blood test could identify them. But a new study, published in Alzheimer's Research & Therapy, challenges that idea, suggesting the reasons for their cognitive resilience are more complex.

The stakes are high. Alzheimer's disease is a leading cause of dementia, and as the population ages, finding ways to preserve memory is more urgent than ever. SuperAgers offer a living proof that cognitive decline is not inevitable, and their study may reveal pathways to healthier aging for everyone.

## What the study found

Researchers analyzed DNA from 142 SuperAgers and 89 older adults with average cognitive performance, all recruited from five sites in the U.S. and Canada. They looked at the APOE gene, the strongest known genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's, and also calculated polygenic risk scores that combine thousands of genetic variants linked to the disease.

The results were clear: SuperAgers could not be distinguished from typical older adults based on these genetic measures. They were just as likely to carry Alzheimer's risk variants. Even rare genetic variants previously thought to protect against the disease were no more common in SuperAgers. As the researchers put it, exceptional memory in later life does not appear to result from having exceptionally low inherited risk for Alzheimer's.

This finding was surprising. The team had expected that SuperAgers might simply be at lower genetic risk, but the data showed no such advantage. The study is one of the largest to examine this question, and its results suggest that the genetic basis of SuperAging is not simply the inverse of Alzheimer's risk.

## How to interpret this

This study is important because it shows that SuperAging is not simply the opposite of Alzheimer's risk. The absence of risk genes doesn't guarantee a sharp memory, and having them doesn't doom you to decline. The findings suggest that protective factors—perhaps in the brain, body, lifestyle, or environment—may play a bigger role than previously thought.

But the study has limitations. It included a specific group of volunteers, and the genetic analysis focused only on known Alzheimer's risk variants. It doesn't identify what those protective factors are, nor does it prove that lifestyle changes can create SuperAgers. The researchers themselves emphasize that future work must look beyond disease-risk models to understand exceptional cognitive aging.

The study also highlights a key distinction: the absence of risk is not the same as the presence of protection. Just because someone doesn't have high genetic risk doesn't mean they have the factors that actively support brain health. This is a crucial nuance for future research.

## Practical next steps

While the study doesn't offer a prescription for becoming a SuperAger, it reinforces that brain health is about more than avoiding disease. The researchers plan to combine cognitive testing with brain imaging, blood biomarkers, and measures of sleep, activity, and social engagement to uncover the full picture.

For now, the takeaway is hopeful: memory decline is not unavoidable. Some people do maintain youthful memory into their 80s and beyond. Even though we don't know exactly how, the search continues—and every participant in these studies brings us closer to answers that could help us all age with sharper minds.

In the meantime, the findings support a broader view of brain health. Rather than focusing solely on genetic testing for Alzheimer's risk, it may be more productive to explore the many factors that contribute to cognitive resilience across a lifetime.

## Three things to remember

- SuperAgers are 80+ with memory like 50-year-olds.
- Genetic risk scores didn't separate SuperAgers from peers.
- Protective factors beyond genes are likely at play.
