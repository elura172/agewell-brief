Targeted genetic knockdown in mice reveals how inhibitory signaling failures in relay neurons flatten sleep oscillations and trap conditioned fear responses.

To test how inhibitory neurotransmission shapes neural oscillations during sleep, researchers targeted synaptic GABA-A receptors in midline thalamic regions using localized CRISPR-Cas9-knockdown in mice. In a healthy brain, thalamocortical relay neurons generate sleep spindles, the characteristic bursts of electrical activity that rise and fall in a slow, waxing and waning envelope. When the researchers stripped these neurons of their synaptic GABA-A receptors, the physical architecture of the oscillation collapsed. The electrical bursts lost their rhythmic crescendo and decrescendo entirely, flattening into aberrant waveforms that signaled a breakdown in inhibitory feedback loops.

Beyond sleep architecture, this cellular disruption crippled behavioral adaptation. Mice missing these receptors failed to diminish conditioned contextual fear responses, unable to extinguish memories of past threats. While these findings map a direct physical bridge between inhibitory receptor dysfunction, deformed sleep oscillations, and emotional inflexibility, the work relies on a targeted genetic model in mice and leaves open whether identical synaptic alterations drive human post-traumatic stress disorders.

## Key takeaways

- Localized CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of thalamic GABA-A receptors in mice destroys the characteristic waxing and waning shape of sleep spindles.
- Mice lacking synaptic GABA-A receptors in thalamocortical neurons fail to extinguish conditioned contextual fear responses.
- Abnormally shaped sleep spindles act as a direct physical marker of underlying inhibitory receptor dysfunction within midline thalamic circuits.

### Source

Reported from [Synaptic GABA dysfunction of thalamocortical neurons impairs sleep spindle morphology and fear extinction.](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.28.728431) (biorxiv).

> **Evidence note:** These mechanistic insights derive entirely from a targeted genetic mouse model and cannot yet be assumed to fully capture the complex etiology of human post-traumatic stress disorder.
