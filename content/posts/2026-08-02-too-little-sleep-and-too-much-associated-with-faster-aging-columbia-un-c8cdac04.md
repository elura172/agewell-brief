A new study using organ-specific 'aging clocks' finds that both too little and too much sleep are tied to faster aging across the body.

## Three things to remember

- Short and long sleep linked to faster organ aging.
- Optimal sleep range: 6.4 to 7.8 hours.
- Sleep duration tied to depression, heart disease, diabetes.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern study uses organ-specific epigenetic clocks to measure biological age acceleration. This directly connects to Selye's concept of allostatic load: both short and long sleep are chronic stressors that dysregulate homeostatic systems (e.g., HPA axis, autonomic nervous system), leading to cumulative wear and tear. Epigenetic clocks, such as those developed by Horvath, capture the molecular consequences of this stress, including DNA methylation changes in pathways related to inflammation and metabolism. Kleitman's work on sleep architecture is relevant because optimal sleep duration (6.4-7.8 hours) likely reflects the time needed for restorative processes (e.g., slow-wave sleep for glymphatic clearance, REM for synaptic homeostasis) that mitigate cellular damage and maintain epigenetic stability.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer societies, such as the Hadza and Tsimane, typically sleep around 6.5-7.5 hours per night, aligning with the optimal range found in this study. Their sleep patterns are entrained to natural light-dark cycles, with no artificial lighting, which supports robust circadian rhythms. This alignment suggests that the human genome is adapted to a specific sleep duration that optimizes cellular repair and epigenetic maintenance. The physical mechanism involves the circadian regulation of cortisol and melatonin, which influence DNA repair and antioxidant defenses. Deviations from this ancestral norm, whether too short or too long, disrupt these rhythms and accelerate organ aging, as reflected in the epigenetic clocks.
