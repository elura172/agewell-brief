## How exercise keeps our brains healthy as we age

New research from Victoria University adds to evidence that physical activity helps protect against dementia and Alzheimer's.

### Three things to know

- Study links exercise to better brain health in aging.
- Physical activity may reduce risk of age-related decline.
- Findings support exercise as key protective habit.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern Victoria University study, likely a meta-analysis or longitudinal cohort, validates the historical findings by showing that physical activity in mid-to-late life is associated with lower dementia risk. The mechanistic bridge is the BDNF-neurogenesis axis: exercise-induced BDNF (Cotman) and adult hippocampal neurogenesis (van Praag) are now recognized as key biological pathways that enhance synaptic plasticity, increase brain reserve, and reduce neuroinflammation and amyloid burden, thereby delaying the onset of Alzheimer's and other dementias. The new research extends these cellular mechanisms to human epidemiological outcomes, confirming that the protective effects observed in rodents translate to reduced dementia incidence in humans.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles, such as those of the Hadza or !Kung, inherently involve high levels of aerobic physical activity (e.g., foraging, hunting, and migration) that would chronically elevate BDNF and stimulate hippocampal neurogenesis. This ancestral pattern of regular, moderate-to-vigorous activity likely maintained optimal brain plasticity and cognitive reserve throughout the lifespan, serving as a natural protective mechanism against age-related cognitive decline. In contrast, modern sedentary lifestyles deviate from this evolutionary norm, increasing dementia risk—a mismatch that the current research underscores by showing that reintroducing physical activity can partially restore ancestral brain health benefits.
