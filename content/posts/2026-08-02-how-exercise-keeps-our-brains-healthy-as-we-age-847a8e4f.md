## How exercise keeps our brains healthy as we age

New research from Victoria University adds to evidence that physical activity helps protect against dementia and Alzheimer's.

### Three things to know

- Study links exercise to better brain health in aging.
- Physical activity may reduce risk of age-related decline.
- Findings support exercise as key protective habit.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern study links physical activity to reduced dementia risk, likely through mechanisms that involve improved mitochondrial function and reduced neuroinflammation. Warburg's work established the importance of mitochondrial respiration for cellular health; exercise enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and efficiency, which supports neuronal energy metabolism and resilience. Selye's concept of hormesis—where moderate stress (like exercise) triggers adaptive responses—aligns with the finding that physical activity induces beneficial stress responses (e.g., upregulation of neurotrophic factors like BDNF, increased antioxidant defenses) that protect the aging brain. Thus, the current evidence validates and extends these historical insights by showing that exercise-induced metabolic and stress-adaptation pathways are neuroprotective.

**Ancestral parallel:** Traditional hunter-gatherer lifestyles inherently involved regular physical activity (e.g., hunting, gathering, migration) as a normal part of daily life. This pattern of intermittent moderate-to-vigorous exertion would have maintained mitochondrial health and stress-adaptation pathways, reducing the risk of age-related cognitive decline. The modern sedentary lifestyle deviates from this ancestral norm, and the study's findings support the reintroduction of regular physical activity as a way to mimic the protective physiological states that were evolutionarily typical.
