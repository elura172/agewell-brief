New research indicates that circadian misalignment may heighten dementia risk, reflecting chronobiology's foundational principle that human physiology is tuned to ancestral light-dark cycles.



## Three things to remember

- Circadian disruption is associated with increased dementia risk in a recent report.
- Potential mechanisms include neuroinflammation and impaired amyloid-beta clearance.
- Maintaining consistent sleep and light exposure supports brain health.

## How to interpret it


### Historical Biophysics & Lineage
The modern finding that circadian disruption elevates dementia risk directly validates Halberg's foundational principle that organisms are temporally organized and that desynchronization from environmental cycles has pathological consequences. Mechanistically, circadian disruption increases neuroinflammation and impairs amyloid-beta clearance, processes that are regulated by the circadian clock genes (e.g., BMAL1, CLOCK). This aligns with Selye's model: chronic circadian misalignment acts as a persistent stressor, activating the HPA axis and glucocorticoid release, which in turn exacerbates neuroinflammation and impairs glymphatic clearance of amyloid-beta. Thus, the historical stress-adaptation framework provides a physiological pathway linking circadian disruption to neurodegeneration.

**Ancestral parallel:** Ancestral humans lived in strict synchrony with solar light-dark cycles, with no artificial light after dusk. This ensured robust circadian rhythms and optimal brain function. Modern lifestyle, with shift work, late-night screen exposure, and irregular sleep, disrupts this ancient rhythm. The physical mechanism involves light exposure regulating the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and melatonin secretion, which modulate sleep-wake cycles and neuroprotective processes. Traditional practices like 'early to bed, early to rise' and seasonal living align with these ancestral patterns, supporting circadian stability and reducing dementia risk.
